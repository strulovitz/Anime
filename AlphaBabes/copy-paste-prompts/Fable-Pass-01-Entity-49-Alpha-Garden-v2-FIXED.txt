Single full-frame interior: the GARDEN of one submarine hull of the Alpha — not a farm as decoration but the hull's life-support engine: the compartment that makes the oxygen, closes the nitrogen loop, feeds ten women and, in the gentlest engineering of the whole ship, receives them back. No figures. The space: a long compartment running the hull's axis, bare copper ribs overhead, every other surface clean white composite designed to be hosed down and sterilized. Both walls: floor-to-ceiling AEROPONIC racks — bare roots hanging in misted chambers behind clear panels, no soil wasted on what doesn't need it — trays of tomatoes, cabbages, climbing beans on tensioned lines, feathery carrots, dense greens under harvest schedules, and one proud, clearly favored rack of BEETS, red-veined leaves glowing in the light: the borscht never runs out, and never will. Overhead, the compartment's sun: full-spectrum QUANTUM-DOT LED arrays tuned to warm noon, their light physically rendered — irrigation mist drifting through the beams in slow luminous veils, every droplet honest. Along the upper walls, the true lungs of the ship: transparent PHOTOBIOREACTOR tubes of brilliant green algae, fine bubble streams rising through them, and mounted panels of engineered LICHEN — cyanobacteria symbionts, the crew's quiet oxygen factories; in one corner, sealed transparent tanks where nitrogen-fixing cultures turn the air's useless N2 into food chemistry, and beside them a dark, humid mushroom cabinet, lid cracked, gills catching a slice of light. The human layer: e-ink labels on every rack with growth curves and harvest dates — "REAPER'S ROWS" the largest section, her tools docked in silhouette holders with surgical neatness, her small meditation mat rolled and stowed at the rack's end. And at the compartment's heart, given space and dignity by the layout itself: THE GRAVE — a broad, low, circular soil bioreactor tank in brushed stainless steel, dark rich earth inside, the one place aboard where soil is allowed, because this soil is the crew: from it rises the garden's richest growth — sunflowers, dill, one small carefully-pruned apple tree — and set flush into the rim, a laser-etched band: TEN names, each followed by a long row of small dates, some rows forty entries deep. Generations of returned bodies feeding centuries of harvests. Fresh-cut flowers lie on the rim — placed in gratitude, not grief. A slim white crew robot stands mid-task at a rack, watering wand extended, unhurried. Lighting: warm grow-light key from above, green bounce filling every shadow, mist glow. Mood: the closed circle made visible and made beautiful — nothing wasted, everything loved, everyone coming back.
