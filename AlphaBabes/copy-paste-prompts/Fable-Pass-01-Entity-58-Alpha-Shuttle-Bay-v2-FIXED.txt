Pixar/Disney 3D animation style, cinematic feature-film quality render. Warm, charming, emotionally expressive characters with the signature polished "Pixar finish" — but NOT a toy world: realistic proportions, real physical materials (brushed copper, scuffed metal, woven fabric, ice, dust), physically accurate lighting with soft global illumination, shallow cinematic depth of field. Hard science fiction: every technical detail scientifically plausible, no fantasy glow, no magic. Original design — must not resemble any existing anime or movie franchise. 16:9 widescreen, ultra-detailed, movie-still composition.

Single full-frame interior: the SHUTTLE BAY of the manned submarine hull of the Alpha — a dorsal dry-dock compartment, the naval submarine's dry deck shelter re-imagined for space, prepped for tomorrow's descent, no figures. The space: the largest open compartment in the hull, bare copper ribs arching overhead, closed above by long CLAMSHELL doors whose seals are furred with frost, one thin, perfectly straight line of starlight burning at the seam — the only star in the room. At center, riding in a cradle of padded shock struts: the LANDER — this hull's TITANIUM mini-shuttle, one generation past today's reusable rockets and shaped by the same logic: a stout, dense, honest machine, vertical-landing legs folded up against its flanks like a resting insect, heat shield scorch-feathered in soft fans around its rim from past atmospheres — each fan a different world's chemistry — grid fins folded flat, and NO windows anywhere on it: sensor domes and camera pods instead, because it lands on instruments, not on courage. An umbilical is plugged into its flank, breathing faint frost down its own line: cryogenic propellant topped off, fueled and ready, the machine equivalent of packed bags by the door. Along one wall, the EVA gallery, and the arithmetic of the ship visible in it: FIVE suit alcoves — half the crew lives in this hull, and never, in twelve hundred years, all ten in one place — four white hard-torso suits standing in their alcoves like patient ghosts, each with its owner's small printed emblem on the chest plate, umbilicals coiled at their feet; and one alcove EMPTY, its suit checked out, its tether hook still swinging very slightly: someone is outside right now. Below the suits, a helmet shelf: gold visors catching the work lights in a row of small suns. The working floor, rendered with love for procedure: yellow guide lines worn grey down their centers by centuries of the same boots, recessed tie-down rings in a precise grid, a robotic GANTRY ARM parked along its ceiling rail with its lifting cradle stowed, a tool cart docked beside the lander — torque tool still lying on the top tray, mid-checklist — and cargo nets bulging with labeled sample crates lashed against the far wall, stenciled by hand with planet names. High on the wall, a wide display: the log of past landings — worlds and dates, centuries between them, the list short enough to read and heavy enough to feel, generous blank space left below for more. Lighting: cool work floods from the ceiling trusses, the hard white star-line at the door seam, frost breath drifting through the beams. Mood: held breath — the quiet of a room that opens onto everything.
